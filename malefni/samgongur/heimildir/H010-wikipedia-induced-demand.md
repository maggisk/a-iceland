# Induced demand — fræðihugtak í samgönguhagfræði

**Lýsing:** Wikipedia-grein um hugtakið "induced demand" (skapaður eftirspurn) sem er grundvallarhugtak í samgönguhagfræði og lykilrök gegn því að leysa umferðartafir með breikkun vega. Notuð til að setja Borgarlínu/stofnvegahliðina í fræðilegt samhengi.

## Tilvitnanir til notkunar

- "an increase in supply results in a decline in price and an increase in consumption" — grunnskilgreining
- "a 10 percent increase in lane miles induces an immediate 4 percent increase in vehicle miles travelled, which climbs to 10 percent in a few years" — 2004 meta-analýsa
- "around eighty percent gets absorbed or depleted" — Cervero rannsókn um California freeway útþenslu
- "nothing but a cosmetic effect" — 1989 Southern California Association of Governments niðurstaða um afkastagetuaukningu

## Greining

### Helstu fullyrðingar

- Induced demand: þegar afkastageta vega er aukin, lækkar "almennur kostnaður" ferða (tími, fyrirhöfn), sem leiðir til fleiri ferða sem annars hefðu ekki orðið.
- Aðgreining á tveimur tengdum hugtökum: **latent demand** er bæld eftirspurn vegna afkastageta-takmarkana; **induced demand** er raunverulegar ferðir sem koma fram þegar takmörkin eru fjarlægð.
- 2004 meta-analýsa: 10% aukning á akreina-mílum gefur strax 4% aukningu á eknum mílum, vex í 10% innan fárra ára.
- Goodwin (1996) yfirlit: eftirspurnar-teygja vegna ferðatímabats: −0,5 (skammtíma) til −1,0 (langtíma).
- Robert Cervero rannsókn í California: u.þ.b. 80% af nýrri afkastagetu "frásogast" innan 6–8 ára; helmingur þess vegna induced demand.
- Klassísk dæmi: brýr Roberts Moses í New York (1930–40), Doncaster Bypass (A1(M), 1961), Southern California Association of Governments 1989 niðurstaða.
- Stjórntækjarök: Steven Polzin mælir með veggjöldum (congestion pricing) frekar en að neita afkastagetuaukningu.
- Wikipedia-greinin fjallar lítið um beitingu induced demand-kenningarinnar á almenningssamgöngur.

### Aðferð og umfang

Yfirlitsgrein með tilvísunum í empíríska bókmenntir. Ekki frumheimild en gefur aðgang að lykilrannsóknum (Duranton & Turner, Cervero, Goodwin).

### Hvað heimildin segir EKKI

- Engin sérstök umfjöllun um norræn lönd eða Ísland.
- Engin tölfræði um induced demand á almenningssamgöngum (þ.e. hvort fleiri ferðir verði til þegar þjónusta er bætt).
- Engin tengin við Reykjavík sérstaklega.

### Tengingar við aðrar heimildir

- Setur fræðilegan grundvöll fyrir öllu málinu — sérstaklega gagnvart H001 ([Stjórnarráðið](H001-samgongusattmali-stjornarradid.md)) þar sem 42% fjárfestingar fara í stofnvegi.

## Fullt efni

### Definition
Induced demand in transportation refers to the phenomenon where "an increase in supply results in a decline in price and an increase in consumption." More specifically, when road capacity expands, the reduced travel time lowers the generalized cost of journeys, prompting additional trips that wouldn't otherwise occur.

The distinction between related terms matters: latent demand represents suppressed travel constrained by capacity limits, while induced demand describes the actual trips generated once those constraints are removed.

### Evidence Base
A 2004 meta-analysis found that "a 10 percent increase in lane miles induces an immediate 4 percent increase in vehicle miles travelled, which climbs to 10 percent in a few years." Research on transport elasticity suggests demand responsiveness to travel time savings ranges from −0.5 (short-term) to −1.0 (long-term).

Notably, Robert Cervero's six-to-eight-year study of California freeway expansions concluded "around eighty percent gets absorbed or depleted," with half attributable to induced-demand effects.

### Classic Examples
Historical cases illustrate the pattern:
- Robert Moses's New York bridges (1930s-1940s) repeatedly became congested despite expectations they would relieve pressure on existing routes
- The Doncaster Bypass (A1(M), 1961) generated substantial new traffic
- Southern California Association of Governments (1989) study concluded capacity additions would have "nothing but a cosmetic effect"

### Policy Implications
The evidence creates tension: while induced demand complicates congestion-relief strategies, some analysts argue certain forms represent welfare improvements. Steven Polzin advocates highway pricing (tolls, congestion pricing) as preferable to capacity denial, generating revenue for transit subsidies.

### Transit Applications
Notably, the article provides minimal discussion of induced demand's applicability to public transit expansion, focusing predominantly on roadway contexts.
